What should a good crystal or rudraksha listing show you?

A good listing answers your questions before you ask them. If you have to message the seller to learn the bead size, the bead count, or what is in the box, the listing has already failed its first test. Each detail below is checkable on the page itself. Use the same checks on any crystal shop online — including the shop this guide is published on — before you pay.

Size and bead count, in numbers. A mala listing should state the bead size in millimetres and the bead count. The Clear Quartz (Sphatik) Mala states 8mm beads, and the Rudraksh Mala states 109 beads — numbers you can verify with a ruler and a count when the parcel arrives. A listing that says only "big beads" or "full mala" gives you nothing to check against.

What the item is made of, plainly. The listing should name the stone or seed, and say if it is natural. Natural clear quartz has natural inclusions — small cloudy marks inside the stone — and the Sphatik Mala page says so openly: inclusions are normal, and each mala differs slightly. A page that shows a perfectly clear, flawless stone in every photo and never mentions variation is either selling glass or hiding the truth about natural stone.

What is included, item by item. A puja box, a kit, or a mala with a pendant should list every item in the box. The Laxmi Kuber Pujan Box page lists its contents by name, so you can check the parcel against the list on arrival. If the contents are not listed, you cannot tell what is missing.

Origin, stated honestly. For rudraksha, the origin most buyers ask about is Nepal. A seller should either state the origin or say plainly that it does not state one — and must not print an origin it cannot support. The 5 Mukhi Rudraksha page on Rudra Sacred states no origin and no supply line. It names the traditional deity (Kalagni Rudra/Shiva) and planet (Jupiter) and states 'No authenticity certificate is included with this bead.' The Shipping policy states only that rudraksha beads in the 1–14 Mukhi and Gauri Shankar range are sourced to order.

How do you tell a natural stone from a glass copy?

Honestly: from a photo alone, you usually cannot. Look for the signs a careful seller shows you, and know their limits.

Natural inclusions and variation are good signs, not flaws. Natural clear quartz very often carries small cloudy marks, tiny cracks, or faint lines inside the stone, and a natural mala shows small differences in colour, size, shape and markings from bead to bead; the product pages checked for this guide state this. Glass copies are usually too perfect — completely clear and even, with no variation from bead to bead. If yours varies slightly from the photo, that is natural variation, not a defect — but a bead that is a different stone, a different size by several millimetres, or a different count from the listing is a wrong item, and the replacement terms cover it.

The strongest check is the seller, not the stone. A seller that states its material, variation, and replacement terms in writing has given you three things to hold it to. A seller that shows only beautiful photos and a price has given you nothing — and the listing is the part you can verify.

What should the replacement and refund terms say before you pay?

If the replacement terms are not written before you pay, assume there are none. This is the check most buyers never run.

A written policy should answer four questions: what counts as a fault, how quickly you must report it, who pays for the return, and how fast you get your money back. Here is how one real policy answers them — Rudra Sacred's Returns and Replacement policy, as published on 12 November 2026:

  • What counts as a fault: the item arrives damaged, wrong, or defective, or it is not delivered at all. Natural variation in colour, size, shape and markings is not a defect.
  • How quickly to report: within 48 hours of delivery, or of the expected delivery date for non-delivery, with your order number. Photos or a short video help, but are not a condition for a valid claim.
  • Who pays, and how you get your money back: return shipping for a fault on the seller's side is paid by the seller; claims are assessed within 2–3 business days. A genuine fault earns a replacement, or a refund of the amount paid, to the original payment method within 5–7 business days after the fault is confirmed or the return is received. No refund is paid in cash.

The same policy also states its limit openly: for hygiene and ritual reasons, there are no change-of-mind returns or exchanges for worn bracelets, malas or rudraksha, opened or used puja kits, opened consumables, or items made or customised for you. That limit tells you to check size, bead count and contents before ordering — and this guide's checklist exists so that you can.

What does a certificate actually prove?

Some sellers print "lab certified" or "certified original" on a listing — words that are easy to print, hard to verify, and the most misused in this market. A certificate is only worth something if you can see it: which laboratory issued it, what was tested, and whether it belongs to the item you are buying. Ask for it before you pay, and check the laboratory, the date, and whether the description matches the item in your cart.

Here is the honest position, with our own store as the example: the 5 Mukhi Rudraksha bead is listed with the words "No authenticity certificate is included with this bead." The bead's page states no origin and no supply line; it states only that no authenticity certificate is included. Every item is cleansed and energised as per tradition before dispatch, as the product pages state. That is a weaker claim than "lab certified", and a more honest one, because it is true and checkable. A seller that claims certification it cannot show is making the stronger claim — and the one you should trust less.

No certificate, no matter how real, can prove that a stone will bring a particular result — certificates describe material, not outcomes.
